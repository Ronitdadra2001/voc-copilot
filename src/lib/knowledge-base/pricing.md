# Pricing Angle — Decision Frameworks

Use these when a review issue is about price, discounts, subscriptions, or
"feels expensive" — pricing complaints need pricing-specific diagnosis, not
generic product/marketing framing.

## Economic Value Estimation (EVE) — is the complaint actually a value-communication gap?
Economic Value = Reference Value (price of the customer's Next Best
Competitive Alternative, NBCA) + Differentiation Value (cost/revenue/
psychological drivers the product adds). Many "too expensive" complaints
are really a **value-communication gap**, not a real overpricing problem —
customers judged the price against a cheap reference point because nobody
explained the differentiation (e.g. air-fried/low-cal/Vitamin-D chips
priced 20% above regular chips failed on price perception despite people
liking the product, because the extra value was never explained). Before
recommending a price cut, check whether the real fix is explaining value
better, not lowering the number.

## Price Fences — is a specific segment being charged unfairly, or just unclearly?
7 ways prices legitimately vary: Who buys, Where, When, How much, Bundling,
Tie-in/metering, Product design. A pricing complaint often names one of
these implicitly (e.g. "why does the same order cost more through the
app") — identify which fence is in play before recommending a fix.

## Price Metric — is the unit being charged for the actual source of the complaint?
The metric is the UNIT price attaches to (per order, per km, per month).
Complaints about delivery fees, surge pricing, or subscription value are
often really a metric problem, not a price-level problem — Netflix's shift
from per-rental+late-fees to flat monthly, or GE's shift from per-engine to
per-engine-hour, both fixed the complaint by changing what was charged for,
not the number.

## Weber-Fechner Effect & framing — is the complaint about the SIZE of a change, not the price itself?
Customers notice percentage changes, not absolute ones — a small absolute
price hike on a cheap item registers much more than the same rupee amount
on an expensive one. Complaints spiking right after a price change (rather
than about the baseline price) point to a framing/rollout problem: was the
change communicated, gradual, and framed as a discount (a gain) rather
than a surcharge (a loss)? Loss aversion means a surcharge-framed change
provokes a stronger negative reaction than an economically identical
discount-framed one.

## The 4-Box competitive-reaction framework — should a price-war complaint trigger a price cut?
Ask: is the rival's price move cost-justified, and is our position strong
or weak? Strong + not cost-justified → Ignore. Weak + not cost-justified →
Attack. Weak + cost-justified → Accommodate (change strategy, don't
price-match). Strong + cost-justified → Defend (respond just enough).
Never default to "match the competitor's price" as the fix without this
check — matching a non-cost-justified rival move can start a war neither
side wins.

## Alternatives to a price war (the 4-box says "Attack" — this is HOW, without just cutting price)
Matching a rival's price cut is the most expensive lever, not the default one.
Cheaper/safer alternatives, roughly in order to try first: (1) **non-price
response** — compete on quality, service, or risk instead ("we're the safer
choice," not "we're the cheaper one"); (2) **selective pricing** — cut price
only on the specific SKU/channel/segment the rival is actually attacking,
leave full margin everywhere else, or launch a lower-price flanker brand
instead of repricing the core line; (3) **change the comparison** — reframe
what's being compared (a bundle/package vs a bare unit price) so the rival's
lower number isn't a clean apples-to-apples anymore; (4) **retreat** — cede
the segment deliberately if defending it isn't worth the margin, and say so
explicitly rather than bleeding out slowly. Only recommend actually matching
price when at least one is true: a real cost advantage exists, the threat is
confined to a small segment competitors won't chase, losses there are
subsidized by profit elsewhere, or cutting price genuinely grows the whole
category (not just reslices it) — the reasoning should name which of these
applies, not just say "match the price."

## Incremental cost, not average cost — is a "give this away free" fix actually unprofitable?
A fix suggestion should be judged on incremental (avoidable) cost, never
average cost. A goodwill discount/loyalty perk that "looks unprofitable"
against average cost-per-customer is often genuinely profitable once you
only count the extra cost of serving that specific complaint segment —
average-cost thinking rejects genuinely good fixes.

## Price Waterfall & policy — is inconsistent discounting the actual root cause?
List Price steps down through every discount/rebate/allowance to the real
Pocket Price customers pay. Complaints about "used to get better deals" or
inconsistent pricing across users often trace back to a discount that was
never meant to be permanent becoming the new expected floor once granted
under pressure — the fix is a clear, held policy, not another one-off
concession.

## Pricing across the product life cycle — does urgency match the stage?
Introduction: price-insensitive, focus on value communication, not price
cuts. Growth: rising price sensitivity as comparison shopping starts.
Maturity: real price-war risk as products commoditize — defend margin via
segmentation/unbundling, not blanket discounts. Decline: retrench, harvest,
or consolidate rather than chase share with price.

## Output discipline
Weak: "Lower the price to fix complaints about cost."
Strong: "Price complaints cluster around the delivery-fee metric, not the
core subscription price (Price Metric) — customers can't predict what
they'll pay per order. Fix: publish a clear, distance-based fee table
(Price Fence: Where) before checkout, not a blanket price cut, since the
underlying issue is unpredictability, not the price level itself."
