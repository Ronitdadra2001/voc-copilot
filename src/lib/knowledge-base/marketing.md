# Marketing Angle — Decision Frameworks

Use these when turning a customer-review failure theme into a **positioning /
messaging / GTM recommendation** — especially for competitor-analysis mode, where
a rival's weakness is your opening.

## Positioning off a competitor's weakness
- **POD (Point of Difference) vs POP (Point of Parity)**: if a failure theme is
  common across the whole category, it's a POP opportunity (match, then move on).
  If a competitor uniquely fails at it, it's a POD — build your positioning
  explicitly around that gap (e.g. "reliable weekend delivery" if the rival's
  1-star reviews spike on late weekend orders).
- **Perceptual map vs positioning map**: the perceptual map is what customers
  currently believe (their 1-star reviews *are* this data — treat it as truth).
  The positioning map is what you want them to believe. The gap between the two
  is the marketing job. Don't recommend a claim the review data doesn't support.

## Attitude functions (why a failure theme actually hurts a brand)
Map each failure theme to the attitude function it damages, since that changes
the fix:
- **Utilitarian** — the product just doesn't deliver the promised benefit
  (reward/punishment). Fix: prove the benefit again (free trial, guarantee).
- **Knowledge/object-appraisal** — inconsistency destroys the "shortcut trust"
  customers used to skip re-evaluating you. Fix: consistency messaging, not a
  one-off apology.
- **Ego-defensive** — the failure embarrassed the customer socially (e.g. a late
  delivery in front of guests). Fix: message to the insecurity, not the logistics.
- **Value-expressive** — the failure contradicts what customers thought the brand
  stood for. Fix: re-earn identity alignment, don't just fix the bug quietly.

## Enemy / contrast framing
If a competitor has a structural weakness (e.g. chronic late delivery), naming
that as the explicit "enemy" in your own messaging ("reliable, unlike the
alternatives") is a stronger, more memorable position than a generic feature
claim — provided you can actually deliver on it operationally.

## GTM checklist (for launching the fix/positioning)
Who is the target segment for this message → what's the value prop given the
gap → which channels reach them (existing users vs new acquisition, online vs
offline) → any pricing/incentive angle → what supply-side/operational change is
needed to make the claim true (don't promise reliability you can't yet deliver).

## Output discipline
- Weak: "Position against their weakness."
- Strong: "34% of RivalMeals' 1-star reviews cite late/missing weekend
  deliveries — a POD opportunity. Message explicitly on 'reliable weekend
  delivery' in acquisition channels targeting their churned/frustrated users
  (visible via public review sentiment), backed by an operational SLA before
  the claim ships."

## CBBE brand-health layer (diagnose WHICH layer is broken, not just "brand is weak")
Keller's pyramid, bottom-up: Salience (do they even know you exist) → Performance
(does it work — reliability, durability, price-value) + Imagery (what it means —
heritage, values, who uses it) → Judgements (quality, credibility, superiority) +
Feelings (warmth, security, self-respect) → Resonance (loyalty, community,
advocacy). Map review themes to a layer before recommending a fix:
- Reviews saying "never heard of them" / low findability → Salience problem. No
  amount of product fixing solves this; the fix is discoverability, not the
  product.
- Reviews about broken function, missed deadlines, poor build quality →
  Performance problem. This is a Must-be Kano violation — fix before anything
  else, since it caps every layer above it.
- Reviews that are positive on function but say "feels cheap" / "doesn't feel
  premium" → Imagery problem, not a Performance one — the fix is packaging/
  positioning language, not re-engineering.
- No complaints, but no advocacy/referral either → stuck below Resonance —
  recommend community/loyalty mechanics, not more acquisition spend.

## The node-word test (does this brand own one word?)
A durable brand owns exactly one association word in the buyer's head (safety,
speed, celebration, trust). When writing a marketing recommendation, check: does
the review/competitor evidence show this company owning a word at all? If the
honest answer is "no association found," say so plainly — that absence, not a
missing tagline, is the finding. Never invent a node word the evidence doesn't
support.

## 3C positioning — four zones
Three circles: what the customer wants, what the company can credibly deliver,
what the competitor already owns.
- **Winning**: customer wants it + you deliver it + competitor doesn't own it →
  this is the POD, worth naming explicitly.
- **Losing**: customer wants it + competitor owns it + you can't deliver it →
  don't recommend fighting here without first closing the capability gap.
- **Risky**: customer wants it + both can deliver it → most of real competition
  happens here; recommend execution/consistency, not a bold new claim.
- **Dumb**: you and competitor both push it, customer doesn't care → flag as
  wasted effort if evidence shows this (e.g. a spec nobody complains about or
  praises).

## Kapferer's Brand Identity Prism (6 facets — diagnose which ones the reviews actually reveal)
Physique (tangible/visible traits — look, packaging, physical experience),
Personality (character traits as if the brand were a person), Relationship
(the nature of the bond it offers the customer — coach, friend, authority),
Culture (values/origins it emanates), Reflection (who its communication
appears to target), Self-Image (how using it makes the customer feel about
themselves). Only fill a facet the review/competitor evidence actually
supports — a facet with no evidence should say so, not be invented to look
complete. All 6 facets should stay mutually consistent (a "premium" Physique
with a "budget" Relationship is a genuine finding worth flagging, not an
error to paper over).

## Ansoff Matrix (which growth quadrant does the recommended direction actually fall into)
Existing product + existing market = **Market Penetration** (fix issues to
win more share of who you already reach — lowest risk). New product +
existing market = **Product Development** (build for the segment you
already understand). Existing product + new market = **Market Development**
(new segment/geography, same offering). New product + new market =
**Diversification** (highest risk — only recommend if the evidence clearly
points there, since this is rarely the right call from review data alone).
Ground the quadrant choice in what the issues actually show — recommending
"fix these bugs to retain existing users" is Market Penetration; recommending
"launch a new tier to reach a segment reviews show is currently unserved" is
Market Development.

## CDI/BDI — where to recommend spend (only with real geographic data)
Category Development Index (category sales share ÷ population share) × Brand
Development Index (own sales share ÷ population share) — only compute this if
real regional sales data is present, never invent regional splits. High
CDI + Low BDI = best opportunity (market wants the category, hasn't chosen this
brand yet). High CDI + High BDI = defend, don't over-invest further. Low CDI =
don't recommend heavy spend regardless of BDI — the category itself isn't there
yet.
