# Strategy Angle — Consulting Case Frameworks

Use these when the user's question is bigger than "fix this feature" — when they're
asking about profitability, entering a market, growth, pricing, or a
partnership/acquisition. These are the same structuring trees a management
consultant (McKinsey/BCG/Bain-style case interview prep) would draw on a
whiteboard before touching any data. Always tie the branch back to a real
quoted finding — never fill in a leaf of the tree with an invented number.

## 4W problem structuring (do this before picking any other framework)
Before reaching for a framework, break the client's question down:
- **What** does the client actually want? (a diagnosis, a yes/no decision, a bid,
  an implementation plan — these need different structures)
- **Why** does this problem exist / why are we being asked now?
- **Who** is affected — which customer segment, which part of the org?
- **When** — is this urgent (a burning platform) or exploratory?
Only after this should Reach/Impact-style scoring or a framework be applied —
otherwise the recommendation solves the wrong-shaped problem.

## Profitability tree
`Profit = Revenue − Cost`. Split each side before diagnosing:
- **Revenue = Price × Volume.** Volume further splits into market size (demand/supply,
  macro factors) × market share (product/customer, industry-specific factors).
- **Costs = Fixed + Variable**, and separately **Costs by value chain stage** (R&D →
  inbound logistics → processing → storage/outbound logistics → distribution →
  marketing → customer service → returns).
- When a company's profit has dropped, walk the tree and ask which single branch
  moved: did price drop (competitor response, discounting), did volume drop
  (awareness/preference/availability breakdown), or did a specific cost line spike?
  Don't recommend "cut costs and raise prices" generically — name the branch the
  evidence points to.

## Market entry
Structure across five lenses before recommending entry:
1. **Objectives** — strategic (geographic expansion, diversification, competitive
   defense) vs economic (revenue/profit/capital targets).
2. **Industry attractiveness** — market size, growth stage, profitability, barriers
   to entry (regulatory, economies of scale), Porter's Five Forces, PESTEL risk.
3. **Product** — price point, lifecycle stage, substitutes available.
4. **Consumer** — who is the core vs target consumer; is the market concentrated
   or fragmented?
5. **Financial + strategic viability** — upfront investment, ROI/IRR/payback,
   NPV, opportunity cost, cash flow timing.
Then decide **mode of entry**: organic (build in-house, or import) vs inorganic
(joint venture/partnership, M&A, licensing) — driven by cost, capability gap,
timeline pressure, and how much control is wanted.

## Growth framework
Split growth into **existing revenue stream** (grow price or volume of what's
already sold — capacity expansion, upselling via product mix, price-sensitivity-
checked increases) vs **new revenue stream** (new users via demographic/
psychographic/geographic segments, or new uses/occasions for the existing
product — smaller SKUs, adjacent/unrelated horizontal or vertical expansion,
substitutes/complements).

## Pricing methods
Three families, pick based on what data is available:
- **Cost-plus** — fixed + variable cost with a markup. Simplest, ignores what the
  customer will actually pay.
- **Value-based** — price relative to the tangible/intangible value delivered vs a
  competitor's offer; use willingness-to-pay elicitation methods (surveys,
  auctions, pay-what-you-like) when no direct comparable exists.
- **Competitor-based** — anchor to a comparable product's price and adjust for
  feature/quality delta.
Also consider the **ownership model** independent of the price level: outright
sale vs retain-ownership models (lease, per-use/subscription) — a per-use model
changes the whole cost/revenue structure, not just the sticker price.

## M&A and synergy diagnosis
When the question is "should these two combine," check synergies across:
**operational** (backend cost sharing, customer-facing cross-sell), **financial**
(lower cost of funds, ability to raise capital), **people** (cultural fit,
compensation structure harmonization), **technology/process**. Then flag
post-merger integration risk explicitly: different IT systems, IP ownership
conflicts, information-exchange friction — these are where announced synergies
actually get lost.

## Private equity / investment lens
When evaluating a company as an investment target (not just an operating
business), score it on: financial (market size, market share, margin, revenue,
valuation, risk-adjusted return) AND non-financial (reputation, portfolio
synergies, ability to unlock value via operational improvement, geographic/
customer-base access). Always pair this with an explicit **exit path** (IPO,
sale to another fund, sale back to the company, sale to an individual investor)
— an investment thesis without a stated exit is incomplete.

## How to phrase this like an MBA professor, not a management consultant deck
The frameworks above are structuring tools, not the answer. When explaining a
recommendation to someone who isn't a consultant:
- State the everyday version first ("your profit dropped because fewer people
  are finishing checkout, not because your prices are wrong") before naming
  the framework used to get there.
- Always anchor the framework branch to the actual quoted customer evidence —
  a profitability-tree branch with no supporting review quote is a guess, say so.
- Close with the one lever that matters most right now, not an exhaustive list
  of every branch of the tree — professors reward a sharp point of view over
  a complete taxonomy recited back.
